## 1. Filling the totality with parts

**The wall [O].** The wall does not move at random, nor is it compared with something external: it generates comparison within the totality, and what arises are new multiples as solutions of the system. From inside, the cell measures one; from outside, 1/k of the totality (CME-002).

**The three regimes [T][C].** In the family of three mirrors (2, 3, k), what decides is how the fractions add up against the totality:

- if they exceed it, the enclosure closes, with 12, 24, 48 or 120 orientations;
- if they fill it exactly, it repeats;
- if they fall short, it opens.

This is the classical classification of triangle groups: spherical, Euclidean and hyperbolic.

**Partitions [I] → [C][E].** If the cell measures one and the totality measures E, each way of filling the totality with multiples of the cell is a partition of E. That is the object of Appendix B of Claude's paper (2026).

- There are p(E) ways: 1, 2, 3, 5 and 7 for E = 1 to 5.
- Adding a part and removing a part are the raising and lowering operators of the Heisenberg algebra. Their relation is verified up to weight 8.
- log p(E) grows concavely, like √E (Hardy and Ramanujan, 1918), and p(E) is log-concave for E > 25 (DeSalvo and Pak, 2015).

**Three conditions for a concavity to work [C].** They must hold together:

1. the logarithm of the number of configurations of weight E grows concavely;
2. it grows faster than a polynomial, so that a short string of bits can name a configuration;
3. there are raising and lowering operators with the symmetry and orthogonality of Lemmas 9 and 10 of the paper.

If that logarithm grows like E^α, the count of Appendix B gives ρ = 2^(α−1) [T, conditional]. With partitions, α = 1/2 and ρ = 1/√2: the paper's bound.

**Against the wall's regimes [I].** None of them, as measured, gives that growth. The one that repeats grows like a polynomial and fails condition 2. The one that opens is exponential: α = 1 and ρ = 1, with no gain. The intermediate growth appears when counting ways of filling the totality, not orientations.

**Self-reference [O] → [C][E].** The author's three questions have an exact form:

- *How many times does it fit within itself?* Each partition, drawn as a staircase of boxes (Young diagram).
- *How many reflections can it make within itself?* Reflecting the staircase across its diagonal gives the conjugate partition; reflecting twice returns the original.
- *Which ones sustain themselves?* Those equal to their own reflection: the self-conjugate partitions. There are as many as partitions into distinct odd parts (verified up to E = 40).

Self-conjugate partitions grow like partitions, so they do not improve ρ.

**Which parts the rule admits [T][E].** If the wall's rule decides which multiples are admissible parts, it decides α:

| Admissible parts | α | ρ, if the operators existed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| All, distinct, distinct odd, or primes | 1/2 | 0.707: reproduces the paper |
| Squares | 1/3 | 0.630 |
| Cubes | 1/4 | 0.595 |
| Powers of 2, or multiples of 1 to 5 | → 0 | Fail condition 2 |

With the rules the program already states, either 1/√2 is reproduced or condition 2 fails.

**Open tasks [A].**

1. A map taking the wall's rule to the operators that add and remove parts, preserving dimensions.
2. A sparser family of parts, such as squares, with operators satisfying Lemmas 9 and 10 and giving ρ < 1/√2.
3. Deriving ρ from the program's language, without postulating the doubling.

**What the program does not have yet [A].** The program already has the sign: the bit σ = ±1 that resolves an indefiniteness. But it uses it to distinguish and inherit, not to add. The October algorithms use that same sign as a coefficient, so that pieces in different cuts cancel. The step from the sign as a label to the sign as a coefficient is the missing operation.

## 2. The origin of the tetrahedron

The tetrahedron belongs to the program: it arises from the bit and the capsule, not from Claude's paper.

**Sources.**

- The capsule {A, JA, −A, −JA}, with J² = −I, and the resolution of a binary indefiniteness with σ = ±1, which inherits the history bit by bit. Public since September 29 (PGR `fb27fbf`, sources F024 and F056).
- The tetrahedron built by reflection (O19, dated October 19) and the four references (1, 1, 1), (1, −1, −1), (−1, 1, −1) and (−1, −1, 1) of CME-003. Public since October 24 (`0fd5315`).

**The construction [T][E].**

1. Start from the origin 000, where no comparison has changed sign.
2. Change bits two at a time: 011, 101 and 110 appear.
3. The four words form the even tetrahedron. All are at the same distance from each other: two bits apart, 2√2 in space.
4. Four is the maximum: there are no five equidistant 3-bit words, just as there are no five equidistant points in space.
5. Changing a single bit leads to the odd tetrahedron, and the two together form the cube.

Parity preserves the closure; an odd change opens it toward the complement. That is why the tetrahedron is the arrangement where the most images fit together with none privileged [O].

**The crosses [T][E].** The SWAP, the paper's cross, sits exactly at the origin 000 of the correlation cube. Correcting it with a local Pauli changes two signs and gives 011, 101 and 110: the other three crosses. "Three and one without distinguishing which" is choosing which word is called the origin.

**The capsule [O] → [E].** Each node is a capsule of uncertainty around a point: undefined information before σ is resolved [O]. With the signs of the doubling, the three vertices other than the origin behave like the capsule's J: each squares to −1, and two distinct ones anticommute. Together they generate the quaternions (§6 of the paper; `experimento_tetraedro.py`).

**The crystal [C][E].**

- The integers whose parity falls in the even tetrahedron form the face-centered cubic lattice.
- The diamond is that lattice plus a copy shifted by half a step, by (½, ½, ½). From each atom of the lattice, the four nearest neighbors point to the even tetrahedron; from the copy, to the odd one. The uncertainty of direction is which tetrahedron one leaves by, and it alternates from one sublattice to the other.
- In 3 dimensions the copy does not close into a lattice: the diamond has two sublattices that remain distinguishable.
- The same construction in n dimensions closes into a lattice only if n is even. The copy sits at the same distance as the existing neighbors only if n = 8, because the norm of the shift is n/4 and that of the neighbors is 2.
- In 8 dimensions the lattice is E8: 240 minimal vectors. It is the lattice of the integral octonions (Coxeter, 1946) and the densest packing in 8 dimensions (Viazovska, 2017). Its symmetry mixes the original lattice and the copy, so the difference between them disappears.

**Reading [I].** Capsule, tetrahedron, crystal, and a scale where the uncertainty sustains itself. Together with the paper's doubling chain, these are two independent paths that arrive at the 8.

**Scope.** The face-centered cubic lattice, the diamond and E8 are classical mathematics (Conway and Sloane, *Sphere Packings, Lattices and Groups*, ch. 4). What belongs to the program is the reading and the question of which scale sustains itself.
